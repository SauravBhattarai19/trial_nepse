What the data says

A study of every trading day from Jan 2013 to Nov 2026, ordinary shares only (with the pre-2018 price-change data repaired; see scripts/price_changes.py), with each result checked separately on 2013–2019 and on 2020–2026 so a pattern has to hold in both to count. Returns are from the next day's open. Reproduce it with python scripts/signal_study.py.

The market decider

On any given day, NEPSE goes where the majority of stocks go. The net share of stocks rising vs falling explains about 77% of the index's daily moves (correlation 0.88 in both periods). Looking ahead, the best single guide is where the money goes: the 10-day share of turnover traded in rising stocks (money flow). It predicted the following week better than any price-based reading: rank correlation 0.18 in 2013–19 and 0.12 since 2020, against 0.16 and 0.03 for the index's own 20-day momentum. Breadth plus turnover tells you more than the price line alone.

NEPSE follows through, but at the open

A strong day tends to be followed by a higher open: the correlation of today's move with tomorrow's opening gap is 0.37 since 2020. From the open onward there is nothing left (−0.02). Close-to-close backtests therefore look far better than anything you could have traded. Every number on this page is measured from the next open.

The AD-line crossovers that held up

Two crossovers worked in both periods. The fast one is the AD line crossing its 10-day MA: after bullish crosses NEPSE did 0.6% (2013–19) and 0.7% (2020–26) better over the next week than after bearish ones. The slow one is the AD line's 10-day MA crossing its 20-day MA: 1.9% and 3.2% better over the next 20 days. Rules built on the 50-day MA looked best before 2020 (holding NEPSE while the AD line was above it earned 18.9% a year) and fell apart after (4.8%). That is why 10 and 20 are now the default lines.

Bearish crosses are the louder warning

Since 2020 the sell side of breadth has been more informative than the buy side. After the AD line fell below its 7-day MA, NEPSE was higher a week later only 34% of the time, against 48% on a typical day. With all five score readings bearish (score 0), the next week averaged about −0.3% in both periods, versus about +0.9% when all five were bullish.

Use breadth to cut risk, not to chase returns

After 0.4% trading costs, no breadth rule clearly beat simply holding the index. What they do is avoid the worst stretches. Holding NEPSE only while money flow was above 50% returned 15.1% a year in 2013–19 and 13.0% since 2020 (buy-and-hold: 12.2% and 13.7%). It did that while invested about half the time, and its worst fall was −19% and −26% against −42% and −43%. Holding while the summation index rose cut the worst fall to about −21% but gave up more return (9.7% and 12.1%).

Textbook divergences fail on NEPSE

In US-style breadth analysis, an index making new highs without the AD line is a warning. Here it wasn't: those days were followed by +3.0% (2013–19) and +2.6% (2020–26) over 20 days, better than average. NEPSE highs tend to keep running. Breadth thrusts and washouts (very low % advancing) were not reliable turning points either. This is a momentum market: weak breadth is a reason to wait, not to bottom-fish.

Slower participation readings faded after 2020

The share of stocks above their 50-day average and the count of 52-week highs minus lows both predicted the next week before 2020 (rank correlation 0.13), but not since (0.03). The average stock's strength against NEPSE never predicted the index at all. They are on the chart (lower pane) as context: they describe how broad a move is, not where it goes next. That is why they are not part of the score.

Mutual funds don't change the signals

Including or excluding mutual funds and debentures moves the predictive correlations by less than 0.01. Fund units track the market loosely (same-day correlation 0.55, against 0.88 for stocks) and are a small share of the count. Excluding them gives cleaner numbers, not better forecasts.

What this data can't see

Even the best reading explains only about 1–4% of the variation in next week's return. NEPSE's big multi-month cycles are driven by liquidity: bank interest rates, the interbank rate, and NRB policy on margin lending. None of that is in price data. If you fit one more input, make it a liquidity series such as the interbank rate. Treat breadth as a risk filter rather than a prediction.

How breadth is counted. Each trading day, every security in data/company-wise that traded is an advancer (per_change > 0), a decliner (< 0) or unchanged. The Advance/Decline line is the running total of advancers − decliners. Turning off mutual funds or debentures removes them from all three breadth numbers; the NEPSE index itself is published by NEPSE and does not change.

Mutual funds trade near their NPR 10 face value and debentures near NPR 1,000, so they are classified automatically (see scripts/securities.py). Data: ShareSansar, refreshed by GitHub Actions after the market closes (Sun–Thu, 15:00 NPT). Charts: TradingView Lightweight Charts.
